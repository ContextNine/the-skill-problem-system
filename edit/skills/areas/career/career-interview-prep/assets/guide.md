## A shape for any story

Use STAR as a writing aid. Your answer can be ordinary paragraphs without separate STAR headings.

- Situation: Give the context and stakes in one or two sentences. Who had the problem, and why did it matter?
- Task: Explain your responsibility and the important constraint. What were you expected to accomplish?
- Action: Spend most of the answer here. Describe your decisions, what you did, why you chose that approach, and how you worked with others.
- Result: Say what happened and how you know. Include a number if you have one, or a concrete observed outcome if you do not.
- Reflection: Finish with what you learned or would change, where it adds to the story.

First capture enough detail to remember the incident. Later, shorten it into an answer you can say in about two minutes. Use "I" for your actions and "we" for collective work. Explain the decisions behind the tools you used. Mark uncertain facts as `To confirm`; an honest qualitative result is useful. You can anonymize names and confidential figures.

## Target role

What roles and level are you targeting? Any companies in mind?

Write a short, practical answer: the work you want to do, the responsibility you want to own, and any specific roles or companies. If you do not know the level, describe the scope instead. This helps you choose relevant stories. You do not need to justify your whole career here.

## Timing

When do you want to be ready, and how much preparation time can you spend each week?

Write the interview date or an approximate goal, then the weekly time you can realistically protect. Distinguish a booked interview from a hoped-for deadline. This answer can be one sentence; it is for planning, not something to deliver to an interviewer.

## Tell me about yourself

How would you introduce yourself to someone interviewing you for that role?

Use a present, past, next structure: what you do now, the relevant experience that brought you here, and why this role makes sense as your next step. Pick one or two achievements that support that direction. Aim for 60–90 seconds when spoken. Explain the connection between your experience and the job rather than listing every position chronologically.

A useful opening gives your professional focus in plain language. The middle provides evidence. The ending explains what you want to contribute in this role. Leave details for follow-up questions.

## Customer / user focus

Tell me about a time understanding a user's needs changed what you built or prioritized.

Choose an incident where something you learned from users changed a decision. Describe the original assumption, how you discovered the actual need, and what you did differently. Explain how you weighed that need against cost, technical constraints, or other priorities. Finish with what users experienced afterward and how you checked it.

The useful detail is the link between the user's problem and your decision. "We listen to customers" is too broad; describe the conversation, observation, support pattern, or other evidence that led to a specific change.

## Success

Tell me about an achievement you are proud of. What made it difficult, and what was your contribution?

Choose a result with enough difficulty to show your judgment. State the starting point and goal, then explain the obstacle and the actions you personally owned. Include a difficult decision or turning point. End with the outcome and why it mattered to the customer, team, or business.

Give collaborators credit while making your responsibility clear. Avoid turning the answer into a list of technologies or claiming the whole team's achievement as your own.

## Failure

Tell me about a mistake or failure you were responsible for. What happened afterward?

Choose a real mistake with a consequence you can discuss honestly. Explain the decision or omission, what you knew at the time, and why your approach failed. Describe how you took responsibility, addressed the damage, and changed your approach afterward. If possible, give a later example of applying that lesson.

A strong answer shows ownership and learning. Avoid disguised strengths such as "I cared too much," blaming the team, or rewriting the incident into a success. If the result remained poor, say so and explain the lesson.

## Conflict

Tell me about a disagreement with a colleague or stakeholder. How did you handle it?

Choose a disagreement about a decision, approach, or priority. Explain what each person wanted and why the other person's concern was reasonable. Describe how you listened, tested assumptions, used evidence, or proposed a compromise. Include the decision that followed and its effect on the work and relationship.

Show how you handled disagreement, even if your preferred option was not chosen. Avoid making the other person the villain or spending the answer proving that you were right.

## Problem-solving

Tell me about a difficult or ambiguous problem. How did you decide what to do?

Choose a problem where the answer was not obvious. Explain what you knew, what was uncertain, and how you investigated. Describe the most relevant alternatives and the tradeoff that determined your choice. Walk through your key actions, then explain the result and whether your original reasoning held up.

Let the interviewer follow your reasoning. Technical detail earns its place when it explains a constraint, diagnosis, or decision. Skip implementation detail that does not change the story.

## Learning & growth

Tell me about feedback or an unfamiliar challenge that changed how you work.

Choose feedback you actually acted on, or a challenge that exposed a gap. Explain the gap, your initial reaction if relevant, and the steps you took to improve. Give a later example showing what you now do differently and what effect that had.

The strongest evidence is a changed behavior or decision. Completing a course or saying you enjoy learning does not explain how your work improved.

## Leadership

Tell me about a time you helped people achieve an outcome, with or without formal authority.

Choose a situation where people needed direction, agreement, support, or an obstacle removed. Explain the shared goal and why progress was difficult. Describe how you helped others understand the problem, made a decision, earned agreement, delegated, coached, or took responsibility. Finish with what the group achieved and how your actions helped.

Make the effect on other people visible. A title alone does not explain leadership, and doing everything yourself may hide how you helped the team work better.

## Time management

Tell me about a time competing priorities forced you to make a difficult choice.

Choose a situation where you could not do everything at once. Name the competing commitments and consequences. Explain how you ranked them, what you deferred or reduced, and who you informed. Describe how you handled the resulting risks and what happened.

Show a deliberate choice and its cost. "I worked longer hours" gives little evidence of prioritization unless you also explain what you chose, why, and how you kept the commitment realistic.

## Review and practice

Read a story aloud once you have written it. Check whether a listener could explain the problem, your responsibility, your key decisions, and the outcome. Cut background before cutting the actions that demonstrate your contribution.

For each story, privately practice explaining why you chose that approach, what alternatives you considered, how you know it helped, and what you would change now. Remember the sequence of events rather than memorizing every sentence.
